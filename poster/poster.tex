\begin{block}{Motivation}

In settings like \textbf{scientific discovery} and \textbf{medical diagnosis}, AI agents must \emph{ask smart questions} before acting---but current LMs are optimized to \emph{answer} questions, not \emph{ask} good ones.

We formalize information-seeking as \textbf{Bayesian Experimental Design} and develop inference-time strategies that guide what agents ask, where they act, and when to explore vs.\ exploit.

\end{block}

\begin{block}{Collaborative Battleship}

\begin{center}
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\colwidth]{figures/battleship-splash-2.png}
\end{center}

A two-player dialogue task for studying information-seeking:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Captain}: asks yes/no questions, then fires at tiles
  \item \textbf{Spotter}: sees full board, provides grounded answers
  \item 8$\times$8 board, 4 ships, budget of 15 questions + 40 shots
  \item Human study (N=42): question-asking correlates with performance ($\rho$=0.684)
\end{itemize}

\end{block}

\begin{block}{Bayesian Strategies}

The Captain maintains a \textbf{belief state} $b_t$ over hidden boards and selects questions to maximize \textbf{Expected Information Gain (EIG)}:
%
\begin{equation*}
  \text{EIG}_\varepsilon(q) = H\!\left(\varepsilon + (1 - 2\varepsilon)\, p_t\right) - H(\varepsilon)
\end{equation*}
%
Maximized when $p_t \approx 0.5$: the question is equally likely to be answered yes or no. Questions are translated to Python programs; EIG is computed via Sequential Monte Carlo.

\medskip

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightblue, colframe=accentblue, arc=2pt, boxrule=1pt, left=6pt, right=6pt, top=4pt, bottom=4pt]
\textbf{\color{accentblue}Bayes-Q} \enspace Ask the most informative question\\[0.5ex]
\textbf{\color{accentblue}Bayes-M} \enspace Shoot at the highest-probability tile\\[0.5ex]
\textbf{\color{accentblue}Bayes-D} \enspace One-step lookahead: explore vs.\ exploit
\end{tcolorbox}

\end{block}

\end{column}
